01 · Editor's letter

Strategy without process is theatre.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann · Founder & Managing Partner

Over the past year I have sat in too many strategy meetings where the slides were beautiful and the work behind them was hollow. Not because the leaders were unserious. Because their process for arriving at the strategy was unserious.

We have spent decades training executives to choose what to do. We have spent far less effort training them on how to do it well — how to disagree productively, how to commit fully once a decision is made, how to hold each other to account without drama. The result is organizations that look strategic and act haphazard.

This issue is a small protest against that pattern. We argue that the HOW is not the soft part of leadership. It is the part that decides whether the WHAT ever gets done.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann

Founder & Managing Partner, MLG.

02 · Feature · Decision quality

The five-minute habit that doubled one team's follow-through.

Notes from the MLG coaching practice

A senior team at a mid-cap industrial client had a problem. Decisions were made cleanly in the room. Outside the room, the same decisions quietly unmade themselves. Project owners drifted. Deadlines slid. By the next quarterly, the team would rediscover the same issues and re-decide them — to the same outcome.

Our diagnosis was unromantic. They had a commitment problem, not a strategy problem. So we introduced a tool we have used dozens of times: the five-minute close. At the end of every meeting in which a decision is made, the chair stops the conversation and asks four questions, in this order:

  1. What did we just decide, in one sentence? Half the time, the room has heard four different sentences.

  2. Who owns it? Not a team. A name.

  3. When is the first checkpoint? Not when it's done — when we will first know if it is on track.

  4. Who in this room disagrees and is committing anyway? The single most useful question we know.

"We are not bad at thinking. We are bad at the last five minutes of meetings."

— MLG coaching team

Six months in, this team's follow-through on stated decisions rose from roughly 40% to over 80%. They did not get smarter. They got slightly more honest, slightly more often.

Try this

Add the four questions to your next meeting

You don't need to announce it. Just bring them with you. At the point where the room is wrapping up, hold for five minutes and ask. The answer to Question 4 is the one that changes a culture.

03 · Field note · Communication

The CEO who stopped explaining and started repeating.

From the field

A CEO we coach made one small change to her quarterly all-hands. She used to share new strategic context each time. She replaced that with something her team initially found insulting: she repeated the same three priorities, in the same order, with slightly different examples, four quarters in a row.

The first quarter, her team rolled their eyes. By the third quarter, those three priorities were showing up unprompted in middle-management conversations across three regions. By the fourth, they were how the business described itself.

Most leaders mistake repetition for failure of imagination. In organizations of any scale, it is the opposite. The message is not the message you sent. It is the message that arrived. If you have to choose between novelty and arrival, choose arrival.

04 · Practice · Team dynamics

How the best teams disagree.

From the MLG team-coaching desk

The cliché says high-performing teams "embrace conflict." We have watched dozens of senior teams up close, and the cliché is half wrong. Great teams don't embrace conflict. They embrace specificity. The difference matters.

What we see in the best teams

  1. Disagreement is aimed at the idea, not the person. The phrasing matters: "I think that's wrong because…" not "you think wrongly because…"

  2. Disagreement is specific. "The forecast assumes X — I don't believe X holds" is useful. "I have concerns about the strategy" is not.

  3. Disagreement is finite. Once the decision is made, the disagreement converts to disagree-and-commit, with a checkpoint for revisiting.

"Trust isn't the absence of conflict — it's the certainty that conflict will land on the idea, not on me."

— Senior partner, professional services firm

Teams that operate this way move faster, not slower. Because the alternative — polite agreement followed by quiet sabotage — eats more time than any meeting ever did.

05 · Long read · Behavior change

From decisions to behaviors: closing the last mile.

MLG senior partners — group reflection

A board can make a decision in an afternoon. Turning that decision into a behavior that shows up in 4,000 inboxes the following Monday is months of work. Most of the failures we see in implementation are not failures of intent. They are failures of this last mile.

Three patterns that close the gap

  1. Bind the decision to a behavior, in writing. "We will increase customer outreach" is not a decision. "Every account manager will hold one external conversation per week, logged in CRM" is.

  2. Make the first checkpoint absurdly soon. We have started recommending fortnightly for the first six weeks. The signal-to-noise ratio is higher, the cost of correction is lower.

  3. Name the behavior you're stopping, not just the one you're starting. Calendars don't have empty space waiting. Every new ritual must displace an old one explicitly, or it won't take.

Watch out for

"Implementation" as a synonym for hope

When senior teams say "we'll figure out implementation later," they usually mean "we'll write a long email and trust it arrives." Implementation is not a phase that comes after the decision. It is a craft that runs in parallel from the moment the decision is named.

Behaviors stick when the cost of doing them right is lower than the cost of doing them wrong. Most "behavior-change" initiatives inadvertently invert that. The leader's job is to fix the incentives, not the people.
