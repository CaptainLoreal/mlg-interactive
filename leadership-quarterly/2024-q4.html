Editor's letter

The year of quiet exhaustion.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann · Founder & Managing Partner

2024 was, in our coaching rooms, the year of quiet exhaustion. The executives we work with were not burning out in the visible, dramatic sense — they were grinding down in a way that few of them admitted out loud. Quarterly numbers held. Energy did not.

I want to use this last issue of the year to push back on the cult of relentlessness. The most thoughtful leaders we know are not the ones who run hardest. They are the ones who design their year so that they can run hard when it matters. There is a difference, and it is worth taking seriously.

Below: a short reflection on the teams we saw recover well this year, and one tool we recommend for the holidays.

Feature · Performance

The companies that slowed down on purpose.

Notes from the MLG client portfolio

We work with several organisations that, over the last 18 months, deliberately moved slower than their competitors on at least one major initiative. None of them did this for ideological reasons. They did it because they had learned, the hard way, that fast and wrong is more expensive than slow and right.

A European bank delayed a digital transformation by six months to redo stakeholder alignment. A logistics firm postponed an acquisition by a quarter to do real cultural diligence. A pharmaceutical client paused a re-org for ninety days to give the previous re-org time to actually take hold.

In each case, the leadership team took external criticism — analyst calls, internal grumbling, peer comparisons. In each case, the eventual outcome was visibly better than what the faster path would have produced. Speed is a strategy. Patience is also a strategy. The skill is knowing which one to buy this quarter.

"The leader's job is not to be the fastest. It is to know what speed the moment requires."

— A managing director, anonymous by request

Tool · Strategic rest

A holiday exercise for leaders who feel guilty resting.

From the coaching room

If you find rest difficult — many of the leaders we coach do — try this. Take a single sheet of paper and answer three questions, slowly:

1. What did I do this year that only I could have done? Be precise. Most leaders find this list is surprisingly short and surprisingly meaningful.

2. What did I do this year that someone else could have done equally well? Be honest. This list tends to be uncomfortably long.

3. What would it take to spend more of 2025 on the first list? This is the real question. It is usually not about hours. It is about which conversations you stop having and which ones you start.

We wish you a quieter end to the year than the year deserved. Back in Q1.
