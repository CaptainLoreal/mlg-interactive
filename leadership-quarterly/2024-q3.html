01 · Editor's letter

Culture is the cheapest moat your competitors cannot copy.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann · Founder & Managing Partner

When CEOs talk about culture publicly, they tend to talk about it the way CEOs talk about luck — as something pleasant that has happened to them. In private, the smart ones know better. Culture is the most expensive thing you build and the cheapest thing you sustain, and it is the only competitive advantage your peers cannot purchase.

This issue presents two case studies from our portfolio — one bank, one industrial manufacturer — where culture was treated as the strategy, not the decoration on top of it. Both ran for over two years. Both worked. And we made enough mistakes along the way that we have decided to write them down, in the hope that you will not need to make the same ones.

02 · Case · Banking

How a 14,000-person bank changed how it disagreed.

MLG client report

A large European retail bank came to us with what they described as a collaboration problem. Two years in, we are confident it was actually a disagreement problem. The bank's people were extraordinarily good at being polite to each other. They were terrible at telling each other the truth.

Our work centered on one shift: making productive disagreement an explicitly rewarded behavior. We changed performance review questions. We trained leaders to ask, not advocate. We worked with the top 200 leaders on what we now call "the courageous middle" — the muscle of bringing up the second-best argument in the room even when the first-best is winning.

Twenty months in, employee engagement on the dimension "I can raise concerns with my manager" rose from 62% to 84%. Decision velocity, measured in actual elapsed time from issue raised to issue resolved, dropped by 40%. The CEO's verdict: we are still polite. We are no longer fragile.

"We did not need to be nicer. We needed to be braver, with each other, in public, on the days it cost something."

— Executive sponsor, retail bank

03 · Case · Industrials

What we got wrong before we got it right.

MLG client report

Our second case is a mid-cap European manufacturer, family-owned, third generation. They asked us to help them modernise the culture. We spent the first six months doing the wrong work. We thought the goal was to make the company more agile. The goal, it turned out, was to make the company more honest about what it actually valued — which was a kind of slow craft that did not need to be made more agile.

Once we admitted our mistake and recalibrated, the work accelerated. We helped the leadership team articulate what they were already good at, in a vocabulary their people recognized. We aligned the operating model — promotion, hiring, recognition — to those existing strengths instead of imported ones. Engagement rose. Voluntary turnover fell. So did the temptation to keep reinventing what was already working.

The lesson, repeated: most cultural transformation projects fail because they import a culture rather than develop one. The right starting question is rarely "who do we want to become." It is more often "who are we already, when we are at our best, and how do we do more of that."

Looking back

What we'd do differently

We would have spent the first six weeks listening to operators on the factory floor, not in the boardroom. The boardroom told us what they wanted the culture to be. The floor told us what it actually was. We needed the second before the first could mean anything.

04 · Data brief · Predictors

The signals that predict cultural transformation success.

MLG science desk — meta-analysis

We looked back over 14 cultural transformation engagements in our portfolio (2018–2024) and asked a question we hadn't asked before: which observable signals at the START of the engagement predict whether it will work? The pattern was surprising and consistent.

11 / 14

Successful engagements had a CEO who could name one concrete behavior they wanted to stop doing personally

9 / 14

Successful engagements began with operator-level interviews before board-level workshops

12 / 14

Successful engagements had a budget for the cultural work that was at least 1% of the strategy's operational budget

The pattern is not subtle. Cultural transformation tends to succeed when the CEO is willing to change first, when listening precedes telling, and when the work is funded as if it matters. None of these are radical insights. All of them are difficult to apply when the pressure to act is high.

05 · Tool · Self-diagnostic

A two-question diagnostic for your own culture.

From the coaching room

We give this two-question diagnostic to every leadership team that walks in claiming they need to "change the culture." It takes ten minutes. It tells you more than most quarterly engagement surveys.

  1. What is something that everyone here knows is true and nobody will say? The answers — even when given anonymously — are the map of where your culture is least functional. The size of the gap between what is known and what is sayable is the size of the work.

  2. What did we tolerate this quarter that, in our heart of hearts, we should not have tolerated? The answers reveal the gap between stated values and revealed values. A culture is what you tolerate. Look at the tolerance pattern and you see the actual operating system.

"A culture is what you tolerate. The values poster matters less than the inbox."

— MLG coaching team

If both questions surface the same theme in your team — that's where the work starts. It is almost never the place a leadership team expects.
