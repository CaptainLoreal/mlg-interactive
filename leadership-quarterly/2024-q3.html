Culture as strategy.

Two case studies on cultural transformation — one bank, one mid-cap manufacturer — and a frank account of what we got wrong before we got it right.

Editor's letter

Culture is the cheapest moat your competitors cannot copy.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann · Founder & Managing Partner

When CEOs talk about culture publicly, they tend to talk about it the way CEOs talk about luck — as something pleasant that has happened to them. In private, the smart ones know better. Culture is the most expensive thing you build and the cheapest thing you sustain, and it is the only competitive advantage your peers cannot purchase.

This issue presents two case studies from our portfolio — one bank, one industrial manufacturer — where culture was treated as the strategy, not the decoration on top of it. Both ran for over two years. Both worked. And we made enough mistakes along the way that we have decided to write them down, in the hope that you will not need to make the same ones.

Case · Banking

How a 14,000-person bank changed how it disagreed.

MLG client report

A large European retail bank came to us with what they described as a collaboration problem. Two years in, we are confident it was actually a disagreement problem. The bank's people were extraordinarily good at being polite to each other. They were terrible at telling each other the truth.

Our work centred on one shift: making productive disagreement an explicitly rewarded behaviour. We changed performance review questions. We trained leaders to ask, not advocate. We worked with the top 200 leaders on what we now call "the courageous middle" — the muscle of bringing up the second-best argument in the room even when the first-best is winning.

Twenty months in, employee engagement on the dimension "I can raise concerns with my manager" rose from 62% to 84%. Decision velocity, measured in actual elapsed time from issue raised to issue resolved, dropped by 40%. The CEO's verdict: we are still polite. We are no longer fragile.

"We did not need to be nicer. We needed to be braver, with each other, in public, on the days it cost something."

— Executive sponsor, retail bank

Case · Industrials

What we got wrong before we got it right.

MLG client report

Our second case is a mid-cap European manufacturer, family-owned, third generation. They asked us to help them modernise the culture. We spent the first six months doing the wrong work. We thought the goal was to make the company more agile. The goal, it turned out, was to make the company more honest about what it actually valued — which was a kind of slow craft that did not need to be made more agile.

Once we admitted our mistake and recalibrated, the work accelerated. We helped the leadership team articulate what they were already good at, in a vocabulary their people recognised. We aligned the operating model — promotion, hiring, recognition — to those existing strengths instead of imported ones. Engagement rose. Voluntary turnover fell. So did the temptation to keep reinventing what was already working.

The lesson, repeated: most cultural transformation projects fail because they import a culture rather than develop one. The right starting question is rarely "who do we want to become." It is more often "who are we already, when we are at our best, and how do we do more of that."
