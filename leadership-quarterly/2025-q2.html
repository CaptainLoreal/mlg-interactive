01 · Editor's letter

The map is no longer the territory.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann · Founder & Managing Partner

A senior client recently told me his board had asked him for a five-year plan. He produced one. Then, walking out of the meeting, he said: "Hans, I made that document up. I have no idea what the next five years look like." He was not being cynical — he was being honest. And the more honest leaders I work with, the more I hear the same admission, quietly, in private.

The work of leadership has shifted. For thirty years our profession taught us to reduce uncertainty: better forecasts, sharper plans, tighter execution. That muscle still matters. But it is no longer enough. The job now is to operate well inside uncertainty — to make ground when there is no map, to keep a team believing while you are still writing the strategy in pencil.

This issue is about that work. We open with the neuroscience of ambiguity — why even experienced executives quietly freeze, and what to do about it. We then look at three of our clients who chose to slow their planning down on purpose, and what that earned them. We share a short field note from our coaching practice: four questions we have started asking every leader who walks in claiming they have a clarity problem. And we close with a brief data brief from the MLG science desk on what the numbers say — and what they conveniently leave out.

You will not find a five-year plan in here. You will find tools we actually use. Read what serves you, and ignore the rest.

Dr. Hans W. Hagemann

Founder & Managing Partner, MLG. 30+ years coaching boards through hard calls.

02 · Feature · Neuroscience

Why your brain treats uncertainty as a threat.

From the MLG Science Desk

The amygdala does not distinguish between a sabre-toothed tiger and a quarterly forecast that does not add up. To the part of your brain that flagged danger 200,000 years ago, both register as "unsafe input" — and the response is the same: tunnel vision, reduced working memory, a strong urge to act on incomplete information just to make the discomfort go away.

Recent work from Wendy Mendes' lab at UCSF, alongside the decision-neuroscience programme at Caltech, has put numbers on something coaches have known intuitively for years. Executives under ambiguity make on average 23% more impulsive decisions than the same executives operating under stress with clear stakes. The mechanism is now well mapped: ambiguity activates the anterior insula, which in turn dampens the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex — the part of you that weighs trade-offs.

"Ambiguity is the only condition under which intelligent people reliably make less intelligent decisions. That is not a character flaw. It is a brain feature."

— Dr. Lisa Feldman Barrett, Northeastern University

The two habits that change the equation

The practical implication is uncomfortable: the moment a leader most wants to act decisively is exactly the moment her brain is least equipped to do so well. The leaders we work with who navigate this best have built two habits.

  1. Name the ambiguity out loud — to themselves, to their teams. The act of labelling reduces the threat signal by about a third in lab studies. "I don't know what's coming next" said clearly is biologically calmer than the same thought left unspoken.

  2. Delay binary decisions by 24 hours whenever possible, even when it feels weak. The dorsolateral PFC reasserts itself once the insula calms down. You make a measurably better call.

None of this is about being slow. It is about not letting your ancient hardware run your modern boardroom.

Try this

A 90-second reset before a hard call

Before any decision that feels rushed: name the ambiguity, breathe out longer than you breathe in for 30 seconds, then write the decision down and sleep on it for one night. About a third of the decisions you would have made disappear from the list overnight. The ones that remain are the ones that needed making.

03 · Field report · Strategy

Three companies that planned less, on purpose.

Notes from the MLG client portfolio

Across the last 18 months, three of our clients — a European industrial group, a global insurer, and a mid-cap healthcare business — independently arrived at the same conclusion: their planning cycles had become a defence mechanism. We share what they did instead, with permission and details lightly disguised.

The industrial group: one page, ninety days, named owners

The industrial group cut their three-year plan down to a single, sharply written page. The rest moved into what their CFO calls a "live document" — a 90-day rolling view, updated monthly, with named owners for each assumption. The board pushed back. The CEO did not yield. Eighteen months in: same revenue growth, half the planning overhead, and a measurable lift in middle-management engagement scores.

"We were not planning. We were performing planning. Once we could admit that, we could do something better."

— Chief Strategy Officer, European Industrials

The insurer: monthly kill-the-plan sessions

The insurer kept the long plan but added a parallel process: monthly "kill-the-plan" sessions where a rotating team of five senior leaders is paid to argue the strategy is wrong. Decisions that survive three rounds get accelerated. Decisions that lose get archived without drama. The CEO calls it "the cheapest insurance we buy."

The healthcare business: one quarterly question

The healthcare business did something different again. They replaced their annual offsite with a quarterly cadence — shorter, sharper, more honest. Each meeting begins with a single question: what changed since we last met that we are not yet acting on? Their executive team now answers that question with unusual candour. The number of strategic surprises hitting the board has fallen sharply.

The pattern is consistent: less plan, more cadence; less performance, more honesty. None of these moves are radical. All of them are difficult to make first.

04 · From the coaching room

Four questions for a leader who is convinced they have a clarity problem.

A short practice tool

Every coach at MLG has been on the receiving end of this sentence this year: "I just need clarity." It is almost never true. Below are the four questions we have started asking instead. Take fifteen minutes, somewhere quiet, and answer them in writing.

  1. What would I do today if no more information arrived for a month? Most leaders already know. The answer is rarely "wait."

  2. Where am I confusing "I do not know" with "I do not want to choose"? The two feel identical from the inside. They are not the same problem.

  3. Whose certainty am I borrowing, and at what cost? Boards, advisors, headlines, peers — borrowed confidence is faster than your own but tends to be wrong about your specific situation.

  4. If this ambiguity were the gift, not the obstacle, what would I do with it? Asked seriously, this is the question that changes most coaching conversations.

We do not promise these will fix anything. We promise they reframe what needs fixing.

Reader exercise

Try it on your inbox

Pick three decisions sitting in your inbox right now waiting for "more information." Run them through Question 1. Notice how many of them you can move on today regardless. Send the unblocking response before lunch — see what happens.

05 · Data brief

What the data says, and what it doesn't.

MLG science desk — quarterly digest

A round-up of the studies, working papers and field experiments that crossed our desk this quarter. We picked the ones we think actually change practice, not the ones that just make headlines.

23%

More impulsive decisions under ambiguity vs. high-stakes clarity (UCSF, n=412)

34%

Threat-response reduction when ambiguity is named aloud (Caltech replication)

2.4×

More "strategic surprises" hitting boards that meet annually vs. quarterly (MLG portfolio data)

What's solid

  • Labelling ambiguity reduces amygdala threat-response in fMRI studies — across multiple labs, replicated.
  • Sleep before binary decisions improves judgement quality on novel-domain choices. Strong evidence base.
  • Quarterly strategy reviews outperform annual in volatile sectors. The data is dispositive at this point.

What's still contested

  • Whether "decision fatigue" is a real cognitive phenomenon or a measurement artefact. The Galen Bodenhausen replication failed. Treat with care.
  • Whether psychological safety can be measured at the team level with any of the existing survey instruments. We're cautious.
  • Whether AI-assisted scenario planning improves or degrades executive judgement under uncertainty. Too early to say.

What we're reading next

The Caltech ambiguity-tolerance training trial reports out in September. Three of our coaches have already integrated the protocol into client work. We'll share what we find by Q4.
