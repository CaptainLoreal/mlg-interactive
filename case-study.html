Case Study

One leadership team, one operating system

How a globally distributed industrial group turned 40 individual leaders into one team with a shared language.

Sample text. This page is a template for project write-ups. It moves from the client’s starting position through the diagnosis and the design of the program to delivery, shared reflection, and the question that matters most at the end: what of it actually made it into daily work?

Client

Industrial group, 12,000 employees

Industry

Manufacturing & technology

Audience

40 senior leaders, 14 countries

Format

Learning journey, 3 modules

Duration

9 months

01

The problem

A strategy nobody read the same way

Sample text. The client came to us with a clear observation and an unclear cause. The new group strategy had been decided, communicated, and presented at every town hall — yet twelve months on, little had shifted in the business units. Projects ran in parallel rather than together, decisions were escalated upward, and the same debate resurfaced every quarter.

The initial request was simple: “We need communication training for our leaders.” We take requests like that seriously — but not literally.

02

Diagnosis

What was actually in the way

Sample text. Every design starts with listening. Over six weeks we ran 18 interviews, sat in on two leadership meetings, and analyzed an anonymous team diagnostic. The picture that emerged had little to do with communication technique.

  • No shared vocabulary. Four divisions used the same words for four different things. “Priority” meant something different depending on the site.
  • Low psychological safety. Disagreement was avoided in the meeting and made up for in the corridor afterward.
  • Strengths without a map. The team was technically excellent but did not know who was the right person for what.
  • Decisions without an owner. It was rarely clear who decided — so nobody did.

Our feedback to the board was uncomfortable and necessary: communication training would have changed nothing here. The problem was not in individual capability but in how the group worked together.

03

Design

A program built for the diagnosis

Sample text. The diagnosis turned into a nine-month learning journey — not a seminar, but a sequence of modules that build on each other with real work happening in between. Three decisions shaped the design:

  • Together, not staggered. All 40 leaders go through the same modules at the same time. A shared language does not emerge in separate cohorts.
  • Diagnostics as the starting point. Each person starts with a strengths profile, the team with a shared read-out. People work on themselves and on the whole at once.
  • Real work, not case studies. Practice happens on the three initiatives already on the agenda. Nothing is invented for the workshop.

The board and HR signed off on the design before the first date was set — including the question of how we would jointly measure whether it worked.

04

Delivery

Nine months, three modules, daily work in between

  • Module 1 — Know yourself. Two days in Singapore: strengths profiles, a shared picture of the team, and the first honest conversations about what gets in the way.
  • Between modules. Peer coaching in fixed groups of three, one 1:1 coaching session per person, materials and assignments on the learning platform.
  • Module 2 — Decide together. Decision paths, constructive conflict, psychological safety — practiced on two live initiatives.
  • Module 3 — Carry it into the organization. Each leader brings their own team into the picture and leaves with one concrete next step.

05

Reflection

What worked — and what did not

Sample text. At the end of every module, and again three months after the program closed, we reviewed the work together: a repeat of the team diagnostic, facilitated retrospectives, and individual conversations. Explicitly including what did not work.

  • What carried: the shared strengths picture. For the first time the team had neutral language for differences that had previously been argued as matters of character.
  • What took longer: psychological safety. It cannot be built in one module — it only took hold once the first open disagreements in a meeting proved to be consequence-free.
  • What did not work: the originally planned digital assignments every two weeks. Too tight for daily reality. We moved to a monthly rhythm after module 1.

06

Transfer

From the module into daily work

Sample text. A program has succeeded when it is no longer needed. To get the insights out of the room, we attached them to routines that already existed rather than creating new formats:

  • Decisions got a name. Every agenda item in the leadership meeting now carries the person who decides — and whether the item is for advice or for decision.
  • Strengths profiles became part of teaming. New project teams start with a short round on who brings what.
  • A cascade into the next level. Each leader ran two sessions with their own team — with ready-made material, but in their own words.
  • A fixed point in the calendar. A half-day leadership retrospective twice a year, facilitated — by us in the first year, internally since then.
